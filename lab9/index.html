Dataset Description

The supplied dataset contains the 50 largest economies by 2025 nominal GDP, measured in billions of current U.S. dollars. It is based on the IMF World Economic Outlook, October 2025 series; observations may include IMF staff estimates and can later be revised. Frozen Natural Earth boundaries provide country shapes and geographic identifiers. GDP and geometry are joined only by ISO-3. Countries outside the supplied 50 are labelled No data, never treated as zero GDP.

2025 GDP Cartogram

Position is approximate geography, shape is a uniformly scaled geographic silhouette, and displayed area represents 2025 GDP.

This is a non-contiguous cartogram. Each top-50 country’s projected silhouette is uniformly scaled around its projected centroid so displayed area is proportional to nominal GDP. Approximate location and shape are retained, but adjacency, geographic area, spacing and overlap can be distorted. Only relative areas are meaningful; the total cartogram area is normalized for layout.

4

Design and Comparison

In the choropleth, the United States and China are most prominent because their dark colours signal the largest GDP values; Germany, Japan, and India also stand out while remaining in their familiar locations. The cartogram makes those same economic leaders visually dominant through broad silhouettes, but it also makes Singapore, Hong Kong SAR, Switzerland, the Netherlands, and Ireland appear far larger than their geographic footprints. Conversely, Russia, Canada, Australia, and Kazakhstan contract because large land area is no longer the visual quantity.

The choropleth best supports locating economies, comparing neighbours, and relating economic magnitude to actual coastlines, regions, and geographic area. Its boundaries, positions, adjacency, and relative land size remain intact, although colour comparisons across a log scale require reading the legend. The cartogram is better for an immediate comparison of aggregate economic size because area produces a stronger sense of dominance than colour alone. That gain comes with trade-offs: scaling around centroids separates or overlaps countries, distorts geographic area, and weakens adjacency cues. Moving from colour to area changes the question viewers notice first: the choropleth asks where high-GDP economies are, while the cartogram asks how much visual global economic space each economy commands.
